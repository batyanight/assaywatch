Each oligo drawn base by base, 5′ to 3′. The shaded region is the one the scoring
penalizes more heavily, and it depends on the oligo. For a primer it is the 3′ window,
where a mismatch sits in the polymerase’s catalytic site. For a probe it is the central
third: a hydrolysis probe is never extended, its 3′ end carries the quencher and it is
cleaved by exonuclease activity, so what matters is duplex stability. Bar height is the
share of records that differ at that position. Bar color is what that costs, measured
as the fall in detection between records that differ there and records that do not:

ORIENTATION, which is worth recording because it looked wrong at first. On that contig Ff642 reads on the plus strand and Ff393 only as its reverse complement, i.e. the opposite of the assignment here. That is simply the gene lying on the contig's minus strand: the primer names encode positions 393 and 642 in the tul4 reading frame, so in gene coordinates Ff393 is the forward and Ff642 the reverse, which is how they are stored. The screener accepts either orientation, so the call is unaffected either way.</p><p><b>2026-11-04</b> THE PAPER'S PROBE IS DELIBERATELY OMITTED. Long et al. detect the product by hybridisation with an oligonucleotide probe, FT443, rather than by fluorescence, so this is a conventional PCR and a probe is not required. More importantly the probe sequence as read from this 1993 scan carries 8 mismatches against the same contig where both primers match perfectly, which means the OCR mangled it. Entering a sequence known to be corrupt would be worse than leaving it out. A clean copy of Table/text would let it be added. CORRECTED 2026-11-04: the probe was not mangled. The page prints FT443 as TCGTAATGTTAGCTGTATCATCATT, read by eye and matching the text layer, and it is exact in all 1,021 products at product position 50, which is gene position 443.
